AI vs. EnginesThe Fate of Horses

Engines are non-jagged across physical tasks

Horses plowed fields, hauled freight and carried passengers. Engines beat horses by about the same factor at every task. As engines got cheaper, horses lost every task at once. AI may beat workers at every task, but by a different factor at each. AI is jagged and leaves workers niches to retreat to.

  1. Producing medicine involves many tasks. Tasks such as R&D, manufacturing, sales and marketing.
  2. Each task can be handled by humans or agentic AI. Human and AI output are indistinguishable apart from cost. Drug discovery, manufacturing, lab testing, clinical trials, sales, regulatory filing.
  3. Each task goes to whoever produces it at lower cost. Unit cost is the factor’s price divided by its productivity.
  4. AI first takes the tasks where its cost advantage is greatest. Cost parity shifts as AI improves.
  5. Workers take up tasks that fall in the AI’s blind spots. Even if AI is better at everything, its relative advantage is smaller in these tasks.
  6. Fewer tasks, but the ones where workers are most exceptional. No task is reserved for humans.
  7. Specialization raises the marginal product of labor. An hour of work produces more.
  8. Each niche task buys a growing basket of AI-produced goods: 1 → 8 units. AI makes the goods workers buy cheaper.
  9. Jaggedness of AI capabilities. A steeper slope means the AI capability frontier is more jagged, with more blind spots. The slope represents AI frontier jaggedness.
  10. The worker task share: the fraction of tasks produced by workers. The solid line is the share of tasks humans perform.
  11. The real wage grows. What an hour of work buys. One hour of work buys the basket of goods: wages.
  12. More jaggedness: smaller ψ. Less jagged: larger ψ. 1/ψ measures the jaggedness of the AI capability frontier relative to humans. AI-driven growth raises the real wage in inverse proportion to labor’s share of economic tasks, with elasticity 1/ψ. Δln w = −(1/ψ) Δln π_L.
  13. What the model predicts. Under jagged AI-driven growth, the labor share falls while real wages rise. Over time, the real wage rises above today's level while the labor share falls below it.
  14. Theory Meets Data
  15. Worker and AI costs task by task. We estimate ψ using data on 484 software engineering tasks drawn from 12 open-source repositories in SWE-bench Verified. Human cost: the time needed to complete the same task × hourly wage. AI cost: the API charge for the tokens used in a successful attempt × 175: deployment costs beyond tokens. 484 tasks, 26 release dates, 6,589 task–date sample. Incomplete tasks provide no AI cost observation.
  16. Distribution of the human-to-AI cost ratio. By the end of the sample, AI completes 75.2 percent of the tasks below human cost, and for the median such task, human cost is 4 times AI cost.
  17. Estimating the jaggedness of the AI frontier. A smaller ψ means greater differences in labor-vs-AI productivity profiles. Less jagged frontier: high ψ.
  18. A smaller ψ means greater differences in labor-vs-AI productivity profiles.
  19. Jagged frontier: low ψ.
  20. The fit implied by each estimate of ψ closely mimics the empirical distribution. Task-level regression ψ = 1.36. Release-level regression ψ = 1.14.
  21. Estimation results. Our two estimation methods yield ψ = 1.14 and ψ = 1.36, so each percent decline in labor’s share of GDP raises the real wage by 0.7 to 0.9 percent.
  22. Historical validation. May 2022: a national survey records wages in 61 sectors. November 2022: ChatGPT is released. May 2025: more businesses use AI, and the survey records wages again. The model explains 2022–25 labor market effects. Each dot is one of 61 sectors: how much its wages grew between the two surveys. Wages grew less in the sectors more exposed to AI growth. Given only AI growth, the model predicts nearly the same slope. The effects of AI growth on sectoral wages were not targeted in the calibration. Goodness-of-fit test (Adão, Costinot, Donaldson, 2025). Goodness-of-fit measure: 0 means a perfect fit. IV coefficient: 1 means a perfect fit. Zero labor mobility: 0.8. Imperfect labor mobility (baseline calibration): 1.15.
  23. AI token costs continue to fall. The annual rate of decline slows from 79.1 percent initially to about 45.8 percent after five years.
  24. Task costs fall by 99.2 percent after five years.
  25. Model’s 5-year Prediction: Real wage rises, labor share falls. Over the next five years, average real wages rise by 18.5 percent while labor’s share of GDP falls from 54.1 to 39.9 percent.
  26. With an AI frontier five times less jagged, real wages barely change.
  27. Jagged adoption is better for workers with mobility. Jagged AI adoption: 8.9% a year. Balanced AI adoption: 5.7% a year.
  28. If AI adoption is jagged, mobile workers will retreat to lagging sectors and demand higher wages.
  29. Jagged adoption can trigger the Baumol effect. Workers in fast-adopting sectors lose purchasing power with respect to lagging sectors.
  30. Every education group gains. Even when workers face large mobility frictions. Five-year real wage gains, no sectoral mobility: high school or less +31.2%, some college +24.1%, bachelor’s or more +12.5%.
  31. Partial sectoral mobility: +28.1%, +22.9% and +14.1%.
  32. Within-sector reassignment. Workers simply sort into niche tasks without leaving their sector. Among the 18 sectors with the highest AI exposure: today, workers 85.2% and AI 14.8% of tasks; after five years, workers 53.8% and AI 46.2%. Even the most AI-exposed sectors retain human tasks.
